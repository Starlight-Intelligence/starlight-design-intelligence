## Transferable design grammar

1. **A scene is an action, not a roster.** Specify who is doing what, with whom, and why. A shared task anchors emotion. Do not force every character into a scene.
2. **Materials carry identity.** Define a per-character material palette and plausible surface behavior: matte ceramic, aged copper, fur, velvet, moss, translucent glass, water, patina. Avoid globally applying one glossy render treatment.
3. **A lived-in world has causal detail.** Objects, tools, traces of work, wear, scale cues, architecture and spatial affordances should support the action, not decorate it.
4. **Emotion is relational.** Direct gaze and gesture toward meaningful scene objects or other characters. Avoid all subjects looking toward the camera by default.
5. **Cinematography follows narrative hierarchy.** One dominant emotional event, secondary activity and quiet negative space. Light reveals the event; bloom and bokeh must not erase design information.
6. **Visual kinship is not visual sameness.** Turtle, whale, robot and Queen can coexist through a consistent world, light logic, composition and interaction while retaining distinct forms and lineages.
7. **Scale-specific editions are designed, not shrunk.** Separate cinematic key art, character identity sheet, ambient pet sprite, tiny UI icon and social crop. Test each at intended display size.

## Production recipes (hypotheses, not original recovered prompts)

### A. Cinematic shared-craft scene
**Inputs:** approved character references; action/relationship; setting; one hero object; aspect ratio; intended publication; rights status.

**Creative brief template:**
> Depict [approved identities] engaged in [specific shared creative act] within [inhabitable environment]. One narrative focal moment: [gesture / discovery / exchange]. Material truth: [per-character surfaces]. Lighting: [direction and motivation]. Camera: [framing, lens perspective and hierarchy]. Foreground/midground/background each advance story. Preserve exact character invariants from retrieved images. No generic lineup, floating decorative particles, duplicate subjects, excessive eye enlargement, universal gloss or unexplained technological props. Leave intentional breathing room for [crop or title]. No new character identity inferred from this study.

### B. Character identity development
Use verified source art for front, three-quarter, profile, expressions, prop interactions, silhouette at 48px, material swatches and movement constraints. Hold face/silhouette invariant across experiments. A turnaround is **not** an animation rig.

### C. Ambient pet and UI manifestation
Define transparent edge quality, per-frame bounds, motion states, idle/working/success/error, accessibility and reduced motion. Require source sprite grid geometry and verified sequence before playback. Small-form edition may simplify ornament but must preserve identity signatures.

## Pairwise evaluation

Compare candidate vs actual reference for one target context. Record concrete observed differences in silhouette, expression, action readability, material richness, composition, world coherence and emotional magnetism. Use **BETTER / WORSE / DIFFERENT PURPOSE / UNDECIDED**, with evidence and counterargument. Do not average these into a fake objective score. Keep rights, identity approval and release as independent non-compensatory gates.

## Quality failure examples

- Beautiful single-character portrait used to justify replacing an unrelated ensemble.
- Mushroom props added to a generic robot without integrating character action, environment or world logic.
- Cinematic rendering that loses recognizable silhouette at UI size.
- Queen's selected personal pet sheet silently promoted to flagship portrait.
- Approved identity overwritten because a new render is more detailed.
- Figma placeholder mistaken for a recovered image-filled source.
- Source hash recorded without restoring and verifying the actual bytes.
